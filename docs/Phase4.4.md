## Overview

The objective of this experiment was to evaluate the ability of the Model Predictive Controller (MPC) to track a predefined sinusoidal reference trajectory while respecting steering and acceleration constraints. The controller optimizes future vehicle states over a finite prediction horizon using the kinematic bicycle model.

---

## Figure Interpretation

### 1. Path Tracking

The upper-left plot compares the desired reference trajectory (blue dashed line) with the trajectory followed by the vehicle (orange line).

**Observation**

* The vehicle successfully follows the sinusoidal path.
* Tracking accuracy is high throughout most of the simulation.
* A small deviation is visible near the endpoint as the controller begins braking.
* The vehicle no longer overshoots the final waypoint, indicating that the endpoint handling strategy is effective.

**Interpretation**

This plot demonstrates that the MPC successfully minimizes lateral tracking error while following the desired path.

---

### 2. Vehicle Velocity

The upper-middle plot shows the longitudinal vehicle velocity during the simulation.

**Observation**

* The vehicle accelerates to approximately **5–6 m/s** during the initial phase.
* The velocity remains relatively constant while tracking the path.
* Near the end of the simulation, the controller applies braking to reduce the speed as the vehicle approaches the final waypoint.

**Interpretation**

The velocity profile shows that the controller maintains the desired cruising speed before slowing the vehicle near the end of the trajectory.

---

### 3. Steering Command

The lower-left plot illustrates the steering angle generated by the MPC.

**Observation**

* Larger steering angles occur when entering or exiting the curved sections of the path.
* Smaller steering corrections are observed while following smoother portions of the trajectory.
* Increased steering activity near the endpoint occurs because the controller attempts to minimize the terminal tracking error.

**Interpretation**

The steering commands remain within the imposed steering constraints and demonstrate how the MPC continuously adjusts the vehicle heading.

---

### 4. Acceleration Command

The lower-middle plot presents the acceleration commands generated by the controller.

**Observation**

* Positive acceleration commands are used to reach and maintain the target velocity.
* Oscillations indicate continuous optimization of the vehicle speed.
* Maximum braking is applied near the endpoint to reduce the terminal tracking error.

**Interpretation**

The acceleration profile demonstrates that the controller satisfies the acceleration limits while adjusting vehicle speed according to the optimization objective.

---

### 5. Tracking Error

The right-most plot shows the Euclidean distance between the vehicle position and the reference target during the simulation.

**Observation**

* The tracking error decreases significantly as the controller converges to the desired trajectory.
* The final tracking error approaches zero, indicating accurate path following.
* The initial error is relatively large because the controller tracks a look-ahead reference point rather than the nearest point on the path.

**Interpretation**

The decreasing tracking error confirms that the MPC effectively minimizes the distance between the predicted vehicle trajectory and the reference path.

---

# Performance Summary

The implemented Model Predictive Controller successfully:

* Followed the sinusoidal reference trajectory with high accuracy.
* Maintained a nearly constant cruising speed during most of the simulation.
* Generated steering and acceleration commands within the specified constraints.
* Reduced the tracking error throughout the simulation.
* Applied controlled braking to stop near the end of the reference path.

---

# Limitations

Although the controller performs well, several improvements can be made:

* Prevent reverse motion by constraining the vehicle velocity to remain non-negative.
* Reduce steering oscillations near the endpoint through additional smoothing or tuning.
* Improve terminal behavior by refining the terminal cost and prediction horizon.
* Evaluate the controller on more complex trajectories and under varying operating conditions.

---

# Conclusion

The results demonstrate that the implemented MPC is capable of solving a constrained path-tracking problem using a kinematic bicycle vehicle model. By optimizing future steering and acceleration commands over a finite prediction horizon, the controller achieves accurate trajectory tracking while respecting vehicle constraints. This implementation establishes a strong foundation for future work involving controller comparison, obstacle avoidance, ROS 2 integration, and GPU-accelerated optimization.
